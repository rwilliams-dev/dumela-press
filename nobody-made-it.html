Priced As If Nobody Made It

Narrated by Chef Dumela

–:––
0:00 / –:––

In August, I wrote that I wanted to know where my tea came from. Not just the country printed on the box. I wanted to know the factory, the cooperative, the people whose hands had touched the leaves before they ever reached my cup, and I meant it. As I prepare for life in Kisumu, where I intend to build something of my own around food and hospitality, I want to get closer to the things I serve. I want to understand what comes from the ground, who grows it, who harvests it, and what it costs the people doing the work. I thought I was asking a simple question about tea. I wasn’t.

Kericho is roughly 84 kilometers from Kisumu, a journey of about an hour and forty minutes by road. The surrounding highlands are among Kenya’s most important tea growing regions. For years, women working on those estates have carried stories about what it takes to earn a living there. In February 2023, some of those stories reached an international audience through an investigation by BBC Africa Eye and Panorama.

More than seventy women told the BBC that supervisors had demanded sex in exchange for employment, continued work, or better working conditions. Some described being pressured into sexual relationships simply to keep their jobs. Others said they had become pregnant or contracted HIV. A hidden camera captured local managers pressuring an undercover reporter in ways consistent with the allegations. The estates had belonged for years to two British companies, Unilever and James Finlay. The tea harvested on those hills found its way into familiar packages, including PG Tips, Lipton, and supermarket branded products.

What makes this story particularly difficult to accept is that the problem was not simply a lack of rules. Unilever had faced similar allegations more than a decade earlier and had introduced a zero tolerance policy and a reporting system. The BBC’s investigation raised serious questions about whether reports made through that system had been acted upon.

Both estates also carried Rainforest Alliance certification. That little frog seal is designed to help consumers identify products connected to farms meeting environmental and social standards. After the broadcast, the Rainforest Alliance conducted audits and found both estates out of compliance with social and management criteria. Their certifications were suspended in May 2023, although tea that had already entered the supply chain retained its certified status.

A label can tell you something useful. It can also leave you believing you’ve learned more than you actually have. I’ve written about shopping for the meat, not the label. A beautiful package, an appealing story, a reassuring seal, sometimes those things become substitutes for the investigation we never do. The question isn’t whether standards matter. They absolutely do. The question is what those standards are worth when the people they’re supposed to protect say they were left without protection.

During a parliamentary committee hearing, Kenyan media reported that James Finlay had recorded 171 cases of sexual exploitation over five years, within a larger total of reported workplace cases involving various forms of misconduct. The company was also reported to have told MPs it had not known about the harassment before the BBC broadcast. I keep coming back to that contradiction. What does a reporting system mean if the people responsible for acting on its reports can still say they didn’t know? What does a certification mean to a woman whose livelihood depends on a workplace where she says she was pressured to exchange sex for the right to earn a wage?

Then the ownership changed. Unilever had already sold its tea business, which became Lipton Teas and Infusions. In 2023, James Finlay agreed to sell its Kenyan tea estates to Sri Lanka’s Browns Investments. The following year, Lipton agreed to sell its Kenyan estates to Browns as well. Both transactions offered local communities in Kericho and Bomet a 15 percent stake in the relevant operating companies. Now the brands could continue buying the tea without owning the estates themselves.

The names on the paperwork changed, but selling a business is not the same thing as resolving everything that happened under its previous owners, and the land beneath those tea bushes carries a history that reaches further back than any modern corporate agreement. The estates occupy land originally leased under the colonial government, reportedly under leases lasting 999 years. Kenya’s 2010 Constitution limits leases to 99 years, but the treatment of older leases has been contested. The National Land Commission sought to have the long leases converted, and a court overturned its order after finding that the companies had not been given a proper hearing.

In August 2026, a Kipsigis community organization sent 30-day notices to Browns, Lipton, and the government, demanding negotiations over returning the estates to community ownership and warning it would go to the Environment and Land Court if ignored. That deadline passed in September. I have found no public report that the case has been filed. The companies changed hands, but the communities’ questions about land, history, and ownership did not disappear with the sale. The people being asked to trust the new owners have their own reasons to ask who had the right to own the land in the first place.

One of the men filmed in the BBC investigation was John Chebochok, who ran a labor contracting company working on Finlay’s estates. Following the broadcast, Finlay suspended him and ended its contract with his company. In 2024, Chebochok was elected to head the Toror factory of Tegat, a smallholder tea factory that also supplies major brands. In February 2026, the High Court in Kericho ruled that he was unfit to hold public office. The court found that institutions involved in regulating the tea sector and overseeing elections had failed in their duties by clearing him to run. The case followed a petition brought by women’s rights groups.

It took years, a major investigation, and further legal action for the issue to reach that point. This is what I meant when I said I wanted to know the factory, the cooperative, and the people. I asked where my tea came from. The answer led me to questions about who controlled the work, who was responsible for protecting the workers, who owned the land, and who got to decide what happened next, and coffee doesn’t escape these questions simply because it comes in a different bag.

In January 2024, the National Consumers League sued Starbucks in Washington, DC, challenging the company’s claim that it was committed to "100% ethical coffee sourcing." The complaint cited allegations of labor abuses, including reporting from the Kenyan tea estates investigated by the BBC. Starbucks had suspended tea purchases from James Finlay in Kenya after the investigation. In August 2025, a judge declined to dismiss the lawsuit at that stage. Starbucks has said it will fight the case. A refusal to dismiss a lawsuit is not a finding that the allegations are true. But the case raises a question that goes beyond one company, what should a consumer be entitled to believe when a business makes a sweeping promise about the ethics of its supply chain?

Coffee brings that question to Brazil, the world’s largest coffee producer. Brazil maintains a public register of employers found to have subjected workers to conditions analogous to slavery. Repórter Brasil has reported that 3,828 workers were rescued from slavery like conditions on coffee farms between 1996 and 2024. That number must be understood within the limits of labor inspections, it represents documented cases, not a complete accounting of every farm or every worker who may have experienced abuse.

Separately, eight workers sued Starbucks, alleging that they had been trafficked onto farms belonging to a cooperative that supplies the company with coffee. At least six of the eight farms were subsequently included on Brazil’s public register. In February 2026, a federal court in Washington, DC, dismissed the case, ruling that it lacked jurisdiction over Starbucks. The court did not decide whether the alleged trafficking had occurred. The plaintiffs refiled in Seattle in June. Starbucks has described the claims as without merit and, in October, asked the court to dismiss the case. Allegations are not findings of fact, but the absence of a final ruling is not proof that everything in a supply chain is as it should be.

The price is visible. The labor often isn’t. Closer to Kisumu, the questions are different, but the people at the center of them are still farmers trying to make a living. Kenyan smallholders deliver coffee cherry to cooperatives and wait for payment. For years, some farmers faced delays stretching to six months. The government has ordered payment within five days, and in March 2026 the president signed a new Coffee Act intended to help rebuild the sector, but even a reform intended to help farmers can become complicated when it changes the way they depend on the institutions around them. Farmers in Kirinyaga pushed back against direct payment arrangements because their cooperatives also provide a way to borrow money.

The problem and part of the solution live in the same place. A cooperative can be a lifeline. It can help a farmer access credit, organize production, and reach a market that might otherwise be beyond reach, but when payment is delayed, that same farmer can be left waiting for money already earned.

All of this is happening on land whose future is becoming less certain. Kericho sits near the Mau Forest complex, an important source of water for the surrounding region. A 2021 Christian Aid report, citing a peer reviewed study, warned that Kenya could lose roughly a quarter of its optimal tea growing areas by 2050 as the climate changes.

Europe’s deforestation rules add another pressure to the supply chain. After two delays, the EU Deforestation Regulation is scheduled to begin applying to larger operators on December 30, 2026, with separate timing for smaller operators. Its requirements place substantial due diligence responsibilities on businesses bringing covered commodities, including coffee, into the European market. Smallholders face practical obstacles in meeting documentation and traceability requirements.

Farmers are being asked to adapt to changing weather, changing markets, and changing rules while trying to earn enough to feed their families. When we talk about ethical sourcing, we cannot limit the conversation to whether a bag carries the right seal. We have to ask whether the people producing the crop can afford to keep producing it, whether the land can sustain the work, and whether the people at the beginning of the chain have a meaningful place in decisions made at the end.

I intend to serve both coffee and tea at my place in Kisumu. I want to know my suppliers. I want to ask where the leaf came from and who picked it. I want to understand the coffee cooperative, the farm, and the route the product took to reach me. I also understand that asking a question is not the same as getting an honest answer, and knowing a supplier is not the same as knowing everything that happens behind that supplier’s gate.

A certificate cannot tell me everything. Neither can a company policy. Neither can a familiar brand name or a carefully written promise about ethical sourcing. Some of what I need to know exists only because workers have risked their jobs, their safety, and their reputations to speak about what happened to them. That is not a comfortable foundation for a morning cup of tea, but comfort has never been a reliable measure of whether something is right.

Then there are the machines. In May 2023, three months after the BBC investigation aired, protesters in Kericho burned tea plucking machines on two estates outside town. One machine was reportedly driven to a trading center and set ablaze beside the road. Police officers were injured, and arrests followed. The protests were not simply an extension of the sexual abuse allegations. They concerned another struggle unfolding across the tea industry, mechanization and the loss of work.

Estates had been introducing mechanical harvesters for years. A single machine can reportedly perform work that might otherwise require around a hundred pickers, although the actual comparison depends on the machine, the terrain, and the conditions of the harvest. A former head of the tea growers’ association, serving on a county task force, estimated that Kericho County had lost 30,000 jobs over a decade. That was his estimate, not a verified count of jobs directly eliminated by machines. The task force proposed a cap, no more than sixty percent of the harvest by machine.

The abuse described in the BBC investigation involved the power to decide who got work, who kept it, and who might receive a better assignment. A worker dependent on that decision could find herself in a terrible position, accept the supervisor’s demands or risk losing the income her family needed. A machine changes that relationship. It also changes the number of people needed to do the job. I have found no reliable evidence establishing that mechanization reduced sexual abuse on these estates, and I will not pretend that it did. The two issues cannot be collapsed into a single cause and effect story simply because they exist in the same industry, but I cannot ignore the question they raise together.

If a job exposes a woman to exploitation, losing that job might remove her from the reach of the person exploiting her. It might also remove the income she needs to feed her children. If a machine replaces the work, it may change who has power over the harvest, but it does not automatically provide the displaced worker with another way to earn a living. The machine can remove one vulnerability without resolving the circumstances that made someone vulnerable in the first place. That is the tension I cannot settle.

Nobody should have to endure harassment or sexual coercion to earn a wage, and nobody should be expected to remain in an abusive workplace simply because a machine might replace them, but neither should we treat the disappearance of a job as an uncomplicated improvement when that job may be the difference between a family eating and going without.

The hills in Kericho still look much the same in photographs. Rows of tea stretch across the slopes. The leaves grow, the harvest comes, and the tea eventually reaches somebody’s cup, but a landscape can look unchanged while the lives of the people working it are being rearranged.

Somebody on that county task force had to put a number on how much of the harvest should be done by machine and how much by hand. Sixty by machine, forty by hand. I don’t know whether that is the right balance. I don’t know what the right balance would be, or whether a ratio can answer a question that begins with the dignity of a worker and ends with the survival of a household.

I do know that the people who drink the tea are rarely asked to think about any of this. We see the price on the shelf. We see the brand. We boil the water, pour the cup, and get on with our day. Somebody else has already paid part of the cost.

— Chef Dumela
